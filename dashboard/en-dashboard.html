Who said it? Detecting false attributions with few judges

Summaries of public hearings attribute opinions to people. This dashboard shows, with real data, the two ideas behind the method: compare the opinion with the turns of the person who supposedly said it, and use that inexpensive signal only to break the ties of the LLM judges, never to overrule them.

1 · the problem

The best match in the hearing may belong to someone else

Inexpensive detectors compare the opinion with every sentence of the transcript and keep the highest similarity. A plausible hallucination is on the topic of the hearing, so that score comes out high.

What changes the verdict is who said the sentence. Comparing the opinion with the attributed speaker's turns, and with the other speakers' turns, raises the AUROC from 0.57 to 0.76 without calling any model.

2 · the judges

The judges' votes tie many opinions

Each judge answers yes or no. Summing the votes of k judges, the score can only take values from 0 to k, and opinions with the same number of votes are tied.

What matters for AUROC are the pairs formed by a hallucination and a grounded opinion with the same score. The fraction of such pairs, τ, is the room a tie-breaker can use: at most τ/2 of AUROC. With all 12 judges, τ = 4.65%, and three quarters of it comes from opinions with 0 or 1 vote.

3 · the rule

Summing scores reverses decisions; tie-breaking does not

The usual approach fits a model on the votes and the features together. A weighted sum can place an opinion with fewer votes above one with more whenever the inexpensive signal disagrees strongly enough. On this data, that model falls below the committee in 19 of 20 fold partitions.

Lexicographic tie-breaking ranks by votes first and uses the inexpensive signal only within each tie. It never reverses a judge decision, and its gain is exactly τ·(α − ½), with α measured on the tied pairs. The 12-judge committee rises from 0.926 to 0.930 in all 20 partitions.

4 · fewer judges

A few judges with tie-breaking come close to twelve

Tie-breaking improves committees of every size, and gains most where judges are fewest, because that is where ties are most common. A typical judge rises from 0.807 to 0.879, recovering about 60% of the gap to the full committee with one twelfth of its calls. Every one of the 12 judges improves, by +0.046 to +0.101, with confidence intervals above zero.

Five judges with tie-breaking (0.923) beat eight without it (0.921). In an exploratory analysis, eight judges with tie-breaking are non-inferior to the twelve-judge committee (margin 0.0040): a third fewer calls. With seven, this cannot be claimed.

5 · measured cost

The inexpensive signal costs seconds of CPU, not LLM tokens

We measured CPU time on a machine with 4 vCPUs and no GPU. The speaker features cost under 1 ms per opinion; the expensive step is embedding the transcript, done once per hearing (≈1.9 s per opinion). The ten NLI calls add ≈3.2 s. The full detector costs about 5.1 s of CPU and no LLM call.

Each judge call reads about 600 tokens. Twelve judges read ≈7,200 tokens per opinion; the eight judges of the non-inferiority result, ≈4,800. Monetary cost was not measured, since provider prices change.

6 · operations

In the review queue, tie-breaking finds more hallucinations

An auditor reviews opinions from the most to the least suspect until time runs out. With a single judge, the queue is almost entirely tied: within each answer, the order is random.

Reviewing 10% of the opinions, the best judge alone finds 40.5% of the hallucinations; with tie-breaking, 52.2%, with the same single call. With 12 judges the difference in the queue is not measurable: the committee's ties sit at the end of the queue.

7 · forecast

The gain can be forecast before the rule is used

We split the hearings in half 50 times. On one half we measure α; on the other, τ comes only from the votes and the labels. At every committee size, the mean forecast τ·(α − ½) is between 95% and 98% of the gain observed on the half the detector never saw, and it has the right sign in 249 of 250 cases.

Using the global AUROC of the inexpensive signal instead of α overestimates the gain by a factor of 1.4 to 3.1.

Each point is one random split and one committee size (1, 3, 5, 8 or 12 judges). Points on the diagonal are exact forecasts (script gain_forecast.py).
